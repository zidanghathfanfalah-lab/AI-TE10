# SATRIA Philosophy — Patient as Customer

## An Irony Too Long Ignored

There is a major irony in the Indonesian healthcare system that is rarely spoken of openly.

Indonesian healthcare professionals — doctors, nurses, midwives — are clinically **on par** with their counterparts in Singapore and Malaysia. Graduates from UI, UNAIR, or UNDIP are no less capable than those from NUS or UM. They go through long education, rigorous examinations, and extensive practical hours.

But why does healthcare in Indonesia feel so different?

Why is it that in Singapore patients know exactly how long they will wait, which room they will enter, and which doctor will examine them — while in Indonesia patients can sit for hours without clarity, without information, and without choices?

The answer is not about competence. The answer lies in **how the system positions the patient**.

---

## Two Ways of Viewing the Patient

In many healthcare systems abroad, the patient is treated as a **customer**. Not in a shallow sense — not just about smiles and neat uniforms — but in a fundamental sense:

> *Customers have choices. And because they have choices, service providers must compete to win their trust.*

When a hospital in Singapore knows that a patient can choose to go to another hospital that is faster, more transparent, or cheaper — that hospital is **forced** to improve. Not because of regulations. Not because of audits. But because if they don't, they lose patients, revenue, and their very existence.

In Indonesia, this dynamic is almost non-existent. Patients — especially those relying on BPJS — do not have many choices. They arrive, take a queue number, wait without any time estimation, and accept whatever is given. Because the alternative is not getting treated at all.

Consequently, there is no competitive pressure. There is no real incentive for healthcare facilities to improve the patient experience. And the most ironic part: **no one is individually at fault**. The doctors are not lazy. The nurses do not lack care. It is the system that has never given them a reason to race to provide the best.

---

## SATRIA as an Instrument of Systemic Change

SATRIA is not just a queuing application or a healthcare chatbot.

SATRIA is an attempt to **shift the power dynamic** within the Indonesian healthcare ecosystem — by giving patients something they have never had: **information and choice**.

### Information

When patients know that:
- Community Health Center A (Puskesmas A) has a 45-minute wait time while Center B (Puskesmas B) only has 15 minutes.
- Their complaints are likely Typhoid, not just a "common cold."
- Symptoms they consider trivial are actually warning signs that need immediate attention.

Patients no longer arrive blind. They arrive with knowledge. And knowledgeable patients are much harder to ignore.

### Choice

When the SATRIA specialist `@clinical-expert` agent routes patients to the healthcare facility with the lowest workload — not just the closest by distance — a natural redistribution of demand occurs. Facilities that are responsive, fast, and transparent will receive more patients. Facilities that are slow and non-informative will lose them.

This is a market mechanism that works not through money alone, but through **data and transparency**.

## What We Believe

**The patient is a customer.**

Not a customer to be treated arbitrarily. Not a customer who must always be "satisfied" even when sick. But a customer in the truest sense: the party who pays — either directly or through BPJS contributions — and is therefore entitled to the best service that can be provided.

**Doctors and healthcare workers are not the enemy.**

They are professionals who should be able to shine in a fair system. Many Indonesian doctors want to provide the best service, but are hindered by a system that does not give them the right tools, time, or incentives. SATRIA is here as a tool — not to judge, but to help.

**Technology is not the goal, but the leverage.**

AI, collaboration within a multi-agent system (`@anamnesis`, `@clinical-expert`, `@compliance-auditor`), and real-time data are not for looking fancy. They are tools to shorten the distance between patients in need and the right help — at the right time, in the right place.

**Transparency is a form of respect.**

Telling patients how long they have to wait, explaining their condition in language they can understand, and not hiding clinical information behind medical jargon — is the most basic way to respect someone who is in a vulnerable state.
